---
title: "The lifeline that nothing was watching"
description: "The VPN back into the homelab went down and nothing noticed, and then the two most obvious ways to check on it both returned confident answers that were wrong."
date: 2026-09-03
---

I rebooted the container that runs the VPN, by hand, in the middle of doing something else, and it did not come back. That part is ordinary. The part worth writing down is that nothing told me. No alert fired, no dashboard went red, and I found out because I later tried to use it. The monitoring on this cluster is the thing I am proudest of and it had a hole in it the exact shape of the one service whose whole job is to be reachable when everything else is not.

The hole is not an oversight so much as a boundary I had never thought about from the other side. Everything the monitoring stack watches, it watches from inside the cluster, which is the right default and covers almost everything I run. The VPN deliberately does not live in the cluster. It sits beside it as its own container, because a way back into the house that depends on the cluster being healthy is not a way back into the house. That reasoning is sound and I would make the same call again. What I had not followed through on is that anything I place outside the cluster for resilience is also outside the thing that would tell me it had failed, and I had placed exactly one service out there: the one I would need first in a real outage.

Then the diagnosis went wrong twice, in the same direction, and both times the wrong answer arrived looking like solid evidence. I was connected over the VPN from a laptop when I started poking at it, and the VPN host itself read as completely dead while every other machine on that network answered normally. That is about as clear a signal as you could ask for, and it was an artefact. The VPN host's own address sits outside the range the tunnel is configured to carry, so a query to it from a connected client never enters the tunnel at all; it goes out onto whatever local network the laptop is actually sitting on, finds nothing of the sort there, and times out. A host that is up reads as down, and only that host, and only when you ask from the one place you are most likely to be asking from.

The second one was worse because it was quieter. Wanting to know whether the house still had the public address it was supposed to have, I asked one of those services that echoes your own address back at you. It answered immediately with an address, and the address was wrong, which looked like a dynamic DNS failure and sent me off in that direction. But the client was split-tunnel, so a request to a public site had never gone through the tunnel either. I had asked my own connection what its address was and it had told me, accurately and uselessly. To get the answer I actually wanted I had to ask from something inside the house, which meant running the check from a pod in the cluster rather than from the laptop that felt like it was there.

The pattern in both is the same, and it is the one the network card outage taught me too, in a different costume: I was measuring the thing I was standing on. The reliable way to check a VPN container is from the hypervisor underneath it, over a path that owes nothing to the tunnel or to the cluster, and that is now the first move rather than the fourth. There is a second question sitting behind all of this that I have not answered yet, which is whether that container is even set to start on its own with the host. If it is not, it combines badly with a fault this container has hit before, where its documented recovery is a full reboot of the machine, and I would come back from that reboot with no way in from outside. And the real fix is the obvious one I have been deferring: a probe that tests the way home from somewhere that is not home, and shouts when it stops working. Until that exists, the last line of defence is a thing I find out about by needing it.
